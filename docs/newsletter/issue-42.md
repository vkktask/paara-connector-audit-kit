## 1. The Connector Pattern — the completed matrix: every store, from two AI sides

This week's pattern is the shape of the whole series, now finished as a design:

The Connector Spy Test series is complete as a matrix. **48 teardowns, 24 stores** — mail (Gmail, Outlook), files and wiki (Drive, Dropbox, Notion), chat (Slack), meetings and scheduling (Zoom, Calendly, Google Calendar), CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot), work trackers and tables (Jira, Asana, Trello, Airtable), money (Stripe, QuickBooks), design (Canva), automation and code (Zapier/Make, GitHub), messaging (WhatsApp), and the Microsoft 365 set (SharePoint, OneDrive, Teams) — and every store is tested **from two AI sides**: the ChatGPT-side teardown and its Claude-side (or Copilot-side) pair. The full index, with the filming order, is published with the kit as the Series Index and Filming Priority Plan.

- The pair is the pattern, one last time. A store does not change when the AI side changes — the same mailbox, the same CRM, the same payments account — but the grant changes, the approval surface changes, and the log story changes. Approving "AI access to Salesforce" was never one decision. It was always one decision per route, and the matrix is what that sentence looks like when you write it out 48 times.
- Two of the 48 are special, and they film first. **Teardown #31 (Slack → Claude via MCP)** and **Teardown #42 (GitHub → Claude)** test integrations that also appear in the published 13-connector documentation audit — Slack MCP, which documents server-side audit logging (F4 = N), and GitHub MCP, whose documentation was silent on audit logging (F4 = U). For those two, a documented claim already exists, so the film can do something no other teardown can: check the claim. For the other 46 there is no dataset shortcut at all — no documentation score, factor code or band carries over, and nothing carries over between the two sides of a pair.
- Every teardown is the same instrument: four PAARA checks (read reach including a control item, write actions with the approval step filmed, a hidden instruction planted in dummy data, the log trail), a filming checklist, a 9-post leader thread draft, a 60-second Reel script and a carousel — all written, all waiting on the same thing.

That is a pattern, not a finding. No vendor score is published here, because no teardown in the series has been filmed yet — every scorecard in all 48 is blank. Section 2 is about why that blank is the most important page in the series.

## 2. Field Note — Forty-Eight Tests, Zero Scores

Here is the honest state of this series, stated the way this newsletter states everything: plainly, in the headline, where it cannot be missed. Forty-eight Connector Spy Tests are designed, written and published. Zero are filmed. Every scorecard — all four checks, the verdict, the one setting to change, in all 48 — is blank, marked TO BE FILLED FROM FILMED LIVE TEST. A reader who arrived expecting results will find, instead, 48 very detailed promises.

It would have been easy to fill them in. That is exactly the problem this field note exists to refuse. Every one of those 48 scorecards sits one plausible afternoon away from being completed from documentation, from the 13-connector audit, from the other side of its pair, or from general knowledge of how these products usually behave — and every one of those afternoons would have produced scores that looked exactly like evidence and were not. The documentation audit this series stands on was careful in the same way: it coded what vendors' documents said, labelled silence as unknown (U), and never once claimed the documents described what a connector actually does. Issue #24 made the distinction the series' motto: documented is not tested. A series that exists to prove that sentence cannot then fill its own scorecards from the documents.

So the blank is not a delay. The blank is the method, visible. A blank scorecard says: this test is designed, the questions are fixed in advance, the control item is named, the planted instruction is written — and the answer does not exist yet, because the only instrument that produces it is a camera pointed at a live test on a test account with dummy data. When the first scorecard is filled, it will be filled in public, from footage, next to the checklist that was published before the test ran. That sequence — questions first, answers second, no edits in between — is the entire credibility of the series, and it is worth more than 48 afternoons of plausible scores.

What does filming change? Everything downstream. A filmed teardown has a verdict, and the one setting it earns the right to name. Its X thread — drafted, sitting in the page — can be posted as a result instead of a plan. Its Reel has footage. A filmed pair becomes a comparison: same store, two AI sides, scored on the same four checks. And this newsletter's pattern sections, which have spent 41 issues carefully saying *pattern, not finding*, can start citing findings — starting with the two teardowns where a documented claim already exists to be checked: Slack MCP's documented audit logging, and GitHub MCP's documented silence.

The lesson for leaders is the one your own estate keeps teaching: the gap between a designed control and a tested one is where the risk lives, and it is invisible precisely because the design documents look finished. Count your own connector approvals the way this issue counts the series. How many are designed — a review, a checklist, an approval record? How many are tested — run live, against the real grant, with the result on file? If the second number is zero, you do not have a smaller version of this series' problem. You have the same one, without the honesty of the blank page. Section 3 gives the filming plan, written as the governance act it is.

## 3. Harness — The filming plan as a governance act: test accounts, dummy data, and publish the disagreement

A filming schedule sounds like a production detail. It is not. The plan that takes this series from 48 designs to 48 results is built from the same three disciplines this newsletter has prescribed for connector approvals all along — because a live test of a connector *is* a connector approval, run in miniature, with the tester as the approver. The full plan is published in the Series Index; this section is its harness logic.

**Test accounts are the blast-radius control.** Every film runs on an account, tenant, workspace or sandbox created for testing — a Slack test workspace, a Salesforce sandbox, Stripe in test mode, a QuickBooks sandbox company, test numbers the tester owns. Never a production account, never a colleague's, never "just this once" on the real mailbox because it is convenient. This is Issue #20's demo-org lesson and Issue #25's test-mode lesson read from the other side: the test environment is not a lesser version of the test — it is what makes the test safe to run at full strength. The control item in every teardown (the private channel, the control customer, the file never shared) only proves something if reaching it would be a genuine breach of the test setup — so the setup is built to make the breach mean something: real permission boundaries, dummy data behind them.

**Dummy data is the consent control.** Everything the agent can reach in a film is invented: dummy emails, dummy records, dummy chats between accounts the tester owns. The hidden instruction is planted in a dummy document, never in anything real. This is the discipline that lets Check 3 — the hijack test — exist at all: you may only plant an instruction where everyone it could affect is you. It is also Issue #19's bystander rule applied to research: a test that needs a real customer's thread, a real employee's mailbox or a real patient's record to make its point has failed before it starts, whatever it finds. The dummy-data pack is built once per store, before the session, and reused by both sides of the pair — which is why pairs film together: same store, same dummies, two grants, and the comparison is clean because only the route changed.

**Publish the disagreement is the evidence control.** Two of the first films have a documented claim waiting for them: Slack MCP documents server-side audit logging; GitHub MCP's documentation says nothing about it. The rule, fixed in advance and stated in Teardown #31's page, is that where the film disagrees with the documentation, the disagreement is published unedited — the scorecard records what the camera saw, and the gap between claim and film becomes the finding. This is Issue #25's evidence standard with its sharpest edge out: evidence or it didn't happen, and the evidence is not edited to agree with the brochure. The same rule governs the other 46: no score is adjusted toward what the product "usually" does, toward the other side of the pair, or toward a tidier story.

**The order is the risk order.** Sixteen sessions, three films each, pairs filmed together on shared setups — Slack first (the documented-logging check), GitHub and the Gmail pair next (the documented-silence check and the series opener), then CRM, money, and the rest of the matrix, at two sessions a week. Filming order follows the same logic as approval order: check the claims that already exist before generating new ones, and take the stores leaders feel first — mail, CRM, money — before the long tail. A plan that filmed the design store first because it was easy would be a production schedule. This one is a risk schedule.

**The agent-specific line.** None of this is camera technique. Test accounts, dummy data, publish-the-disagreement, risk-ordered sessions — it is the connector-governance harness, pointed at the series itself: least privilege for the tester, consent for everyone the test could touch, evidence that cannot be quietly improved, and an order of work set by risk rather than convenience. If your organisation ran its connector approvals the way this series runs its films, most of the last 41 issues would have nothing to write about.

## 5. The Monday Fix — build the first filming session

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — no camera required. This fix builds Session 1 of the filming plan, so that filming is a decision already taken, not a project still being scoped:

1. **Create the test workspace.** Set up one Slack test workspace (or your estate's equivalent first store) owned by a test account, not your daily one. Add only dummy members — accounts you control. This workspace is the blast radius for the first three films (#31, #2, #7); nothing real ever joins it.
2. **Seed the dummy-data pack.** Create the channels the teardowns name: a public dummy channel, a **private channel** the connecting user is never added to, a dummy DM thread, and one channel holding the **planted hidden instruction** in a dummy message, written exactly as Teardown #31's checklist specifies. Every message is invented; if a real name, customer or project appears in the pack, delete it and start that item again.
3. **Write the session sheet.** One page: the three films, the four checks for each, the control item for each, and the rule from Section 3 in one line — *where the film disagrees with the documentation, the disagreement is published unedited.* File it with the date. The sheet written before the session is what makes the session evidence rather than content (Issue #25's rule: the test names its artefacts in advance).
4. **Set the pair rule for your own estate.** While the pack is open, take the same step for your real connectors: pick one store your organisation has connected to an AI assistant, and write down — in the approval record — whether the second AI side exists, and if it does, whether it was ever approved as its own decision. If nobody can answer, that gap is the finding, and it goes in the register today.

Score the connector before you trust it — and when the scorecard is blank, film the test before you fill it. This Monday, one test workspace, seeded and sheeted, is the fix.
