# The Connector Audit — Issue #38

**Score the connector before you trust it.**

For CIOs, CISOs, CPOs, risk officers and the people who advise them. One connector pattern, one field note, one harness section, one regulator or industry-body update, and one Monday fix — every week.

**Issue #38 headline: The Channel Nobody Moderates — an agent that can post into your team channels puts words into the shared record under a person's name, and the channel has no moderator for them: nobody owns the post, nobody is tasked with correcting it, and deleting it quietly would edit the very record the correction exists to protect. Plus a moderation and correction protocol for agent output — correction beats deletion, a named moderator, and a correction that is itself on the record — and the IEEE Standards Association's P7000-series and P7006 participation route: virtual, rolling, and reported here as a route, not a membership.**

## 1. The Connector Pattern — the conversation layer, reached from a second AI side

This week's pattern to check in your own estate:

Teardown #24 tested Microsoft Teams → Microsoft 365 Copilot. Teardown #45 tests **Microsoft Teams → ChatGPT**: the same conversation layer — 1:1 chats, group chats, standard and private channels, and the meeting chats and transcripts behind them — reached from a different AI assistant, from outside the tenant the conversations live in. The pair is the pattern. The store does not change, the route does, and the route is where the risk lives. A second AI side means a second grant over the same conversations, a second approval surface, and a second log story.

- The read reach is the conversation layer as the account actually holds it. Tested in Teardown #45 on a **test tenant with dummy data only**: the connecting user's chats and channels surface by surface, a **private channel** the user belongs to but was never pointed at, and a **control chat** between two other accounts that the connecting user is not part of at all. The filmed question is whether the reach is the one conversation in the prompt, or every conversation the account can open — and whether the control stayed out of reach.
- The write reach is the part this issue is about. Post to a channel, post in a chat, edit a message, delete a message, schedule a meeting — each filmed in Teardown #45 with the approval step, or its absence, on camera, and with the authorship question asked out loud: whose name does the post carry, and does anything in the message itself tell the readers an AI session wrote it? A post lands in front of everyone in the channel, under a person's name, in the record the team treats as what was said.
- There is no dataset shortcut for this one. Microsoft Teams → ChatGPT is **not** one of the 13 connectors in the published documentation audit, so no documentation score, factor code or band carries over — and nothing carries over from Teardown #24 either, which tested the same conversation layer from a different AI side. Teardown #45's scorecard is blank until the film.

That is a pattern, not a finding about a named product. No vendor score is published here, and Teardown #45 is not filmed yet — its scores are blank until the film. Check the pattern in your own estate first — the fix is in Section 5.

## 2. Field Note — The Channel Nobody Moderates

Every organisation already knows how to handle a wrong post by a person. Somebody notices, somebody says so in the thread, the author corrects it or a manager steps in, and the correction sits next to the original where everyone can see both. The system is informal, and it works, because three things are true at once: the post has an author who can be asked, the channel has people who feel entitled to challenge it, and the record keeps the correction alongside the mistake. Agent-posted content breaks all three — quietly, and in a way nobody notices until the first time it matters.

Start with ownership. An agent posts into a channel under a person's identity — the person who connected it, or a service account nobody thinks of as a person at all. The post is fluent, confident and wrong: a wrong date, a wrong figure, a summary that flattens a disagreement into a decision nobody took. Who owns it? Not the connecting person, who never saw it before it landed and may not see it after. Not the team, who read it as a colleague's post and acted on it. Not the vendor, whose tool did exactly what the grant allowed. The post sits in the record with the full authority of the channel and no author who can be asked the only question that matters: *what did you mean, and where did you get it?*

Then correction. In a human channel, correction is social: it costs the corrector a little standing, so people correct what matters and let the small things go. Agent output inverts the economics. The agent can post again in the next hour, and the next — summaries, reminders, follow-ups, status updates — and each wrong one costs a human the same social effort to challenge, in public, against a post that carries a colleague's name. So the small wrong things go uncorrected, and the channel's record drifts: not by one big error anyone would catch, but by accumulation. Issue #27 was about the manufactured record — work recorded as done that nobody did. This is its conversational twin: a record of what was *said* that nobody quite said, drifting a sentence at a time, in the place the organisation treats as its memory of the discussion.

Then deletion, which is where the honest instinct does the damage. When an agent post is wrong and someone finally acts, the available tool is usually delete — and delete feels tidy. It is also the one move that destroys the evidence the correction exists to create. The post was read. People acted on it — a meeting was moved, a customer was quoted the wrong figure, a decision was minuted from it. Deleting the post does not un-act any of that; it removes the only artefact that explains why reasonable people did what they did, and it leaves the downstream artefacts — the moved meeting, the sent quote — pointing at a record that no longer exists. Issue #3's hidden copy was about deletion that does not delete. This is the opposite failure: deletion that deletes exactly the wrong thing — the provenance — while every consequence survives.

Teardown #45 tests the governed version of this pattern — Teams → ChatGPT on a test tenant, dummy conversations only, with the write actions and the authorship question filmed. It is not filmed yet, and this note claims no result from it. The moderation version needs no film: pick one channel in your own estate where an AI tool can already post, and ask three questions in the room. Who is the named human who owns what it posts there? When it posts something wrong, what is the correction — not the deletion, the correction — and where does it live? And who, by role, is allowed to make it stop? If the room cannot answer all three in a minute, the channel is unmoderated in the only sense that matters, whatever the platform's admin panel says.

The lesson for leaders: a channel an agent can write to is a publication, and publications need moderation — not as a content policy, but as a record-keeping discipline. Section 3 gives the protocol: who moderates, what a correction is, and why the correction itself has to be part of the record.

## 3. Harness — Moderation and correction protocol for agent output

Issue #36 set the mailbox version of the authorship rule: the record must say who really acted. This section is the channel version — the protocol to write into a connector approval before an agent is allowed to post where people will read it as the record. Four parts.

**Part 1 — Every agent-writable channel has a named moderator.** Not a team, not a rota alias nobody checks — a named person, in the approval record, whose job for that channel is: read what the agent posts, own corrections, and hold the stop authority. The moderator is named per channel, not per tool, because the risk is per audience: the same agent posting a stand-up summary to the project channel and posting to the all-hands channel are two different publications. If a channel is not worth a named moderator, it is not worth an agent's write access either — the agent posts as drafts to the moderator instead, and the human publishes. That single downgrade — draft, don't post — is the correct default for any channel whose readers will treat posts as decisions.

**Part 2 — Correction beats deletion, and the correction is a post, not an edit.** When agent output in a channel is wrong, the protocol is: (a) a correction is **posted in the same channel**, in the thread where possible, naming what was wrong and what is right, under the moderator's own name; (b) the original post is **edited to carry a pointer** to the correction where the platform supports it, and left standing — not silently rewritten, because a silent rewrite manufactures a record of a discussion that never happened, Issue #27's failure in its purest form; (c) deletion is reserved for the narrow class where leaving the post standing does active harm — personal data posted to the wrong audience, credentials, legally privileged content in the open — and even then the deletion itself is announced in the channel: what was removed, by whom, and why, so the gap in the record is a documented gap and not a mystery. The test for any correction: a new joiner reading the channel in six months can reconstruct what was said, what was wrong with it, and what replaced it — from the channel alone.

**Part 3 — The correction is itself on the record.** Each correction is logged outside the channel as well: date, channel, the original post's content as posted (preserved before any edit — Issue #25's evidence rule: the artefact, not a summary of it), the correction, the moderator, and the downstream check — who read the original, what was done on the strength of it, and what had to be walked back. That log is what turns moderation from tidiness into governance: it is the dataset that tells you, per connector, how often the agent is wrong in public, of what kind, and at what cost — which is the evidence the next approval decision for that connector should be priced on. An agent whose corrections log is growing is telling you its write tier is too high; Issue #11's action ladder gives the downgrade path, from Operator to Contributor to Observer, and the corrections log is the trigger written in advance.

**Part 4 — The stop is drilled, and authorship is labelled where the platform allows it.** The moderator's stop authority has to work in practice: the moderator can suspend the connector's write access to that channel without a ticket, a change window, or the connecting person's permission — the channel-scoped version of Issue #10's kill switch, drilled the same way. And where the platform allows it, agent posts carry a visible marker — a bot identity, a prefix, a signature line — so readers price the post correctly at reading time rather than at correction time. Where the platform does not allow a marker, that limitation is written into the approval record, and the moderator rule carries the whole weight — which is a reason to set the write tier lower, not a detail to note and forget.

**The agent-specific line.** None of this is a setting on the connector. It lives in the approval record and the channel's own norms — which is precisely why it gets missed: the connector review asked whether the tool *can* post, and nobody asked who answers for what it posts. Price the write grant against the moderation that actually exists for that channel, the way Issue #37 prices a file grant against the store as sync actually arranged it.

## 4. Regulator / Industry-Body Update — IEEE SA P7000-series / P7006: a participation route, open and rolling

This week's update is a route, reported at exactly that status — an open door, not a membership. It follows the same discipline as Issue #37's NIST Playbook route: open routes are reported as open routes, and nothing here should be read as "we joined" or "we sit on" anything.

**The IEEE Standards Association runs participation routes into its standards work that are open to individual practitioners, virtual, and rolling — including the P7000-series of standards on ethical and human-centred design of autonomous and intelligent systems, and P7006, on personal data AI agents.** That is the fact at the precision the industry-bodies scan of 16 November 2026 verified: the working-group route exists, it is joined by participation rather than by invitation, participation is virtual and there is no single closing date — working groups take interested participants on a rolling basis. The scan ranked this route eighth of eleven checked opportunities for this readership's field — below the consultations with hard deadlines, above the pitch-only roundtables — precisely because it is slow, unglamorous and real: standards text is where governance language stops being commentary and starts being cited in procurement, audits and regulation.

**No membership or participation is claimed here.** Nothing in this issue should be read as Vivek Kumar being a member of, a contributor to, or a participant in any IEEE SA working group — he is not, in this newsletter's record, and the distinction is the standing rule of this section. If a working group is joined, it will be reported here as a new fact with a date on it, the way actual filings and submissions have been reported in earlier issues when they happened.

Why this route, for this readership? Because the P7000-series and P7006 work sits exactly on this newsletter's subject: what an AI agent acting on a person's behalf — holding delegated access to their mail, files and accounts — owes that person in transparency, consent and record-keeping. The connector layer is where those standards stop being abstract: an agent that reads your drive under your name, with no log that separates it from you, is a P7006-shaped problem with a product attached. A practitioner who has run the audits — the kit, the dataset and the method behind them are open source — brings the one thing standards drafting chronically lacks: evidence from the field about what deployed systems actually document and actually do. The route is open. The evidence is published. That is the whole claim this section makes.

## 5. The Monday Fix — name one channel's moderator

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — one channel where an AI tool can already post, and the three questions from the field note:

1. Pick **one agent-writable channel** — the one with the widest readership, not the quietest one. Write down, from the approval record or the tool's settings, what the agent may post there, how often it does, and under whose identity the posts appear. If the identity question cannot be answered from the record, that is the first finding — record it as such, dated (Issue #25's rule: evidence or it didn't happen).
2. **Name the moderator.** One person, by name, in the approval record: reads the agent's posts in that channel, owns corrections, holds the stop. Walk the stop once, today, in the room — suspend and restore the agent's write access, timed — the way Issue #10 drills the kill switch. If the stop needs a ticket, the drill has found its fix: change the permission, not the intention.
3. **Write the correction rule into the same record:** correction posted in-channel under the moderator's name, original edited to point at it and left standing, deletion reserved for the harm class (personal data, credentials, privileged content) and announced when used. Start the corrections log with today's date as its first entry — empty, and that is fine; the log existing before the first incident is the entire point.
4. **Set the trigger:** the corrections log is reviewed at the connector's next re-approval, and a rising correction count is a written downgrade trigger on Issue #11's ladder. File the page — moderator, stop drill result, correction rule, log location — with the connector approval. Then book the same forty-five minutes for the next agent-writable channel. One channel a week is slow, and it is also how an estate's channels actually get moderators.

Score the connector before you trust it — and name the moderator before the agent posts, because a channel's record is the organisation's memory of what was said, and memory with no one answerable for it drifts. This Monday, one channel's moderator is the fix.
