## 1. The Connector Pattern — the automation layer, reached from a second AI side

This week's pattern to check in your own estate:

Teardown #19 tested Zapier / Make → ChatGPT. Teardown #47 tests **Zapier / Make → Claude**: the same automation account — the Zaps and scenarios, their triggers, steps and field mappings, the connected apps behind them, and the run history of everything those automations have already moved — reached from a different AI assistant. The pair is the pattern. The wiring does not change, the route does, and the route is where the risk lives. A second AI side means a second grant over the wiring diagram of the business, a second approval surface, and a second log story.

- The read reach is the business as it actually runs. Tested in Teardown #47 on a **test account with dummy Zaps and dummy data only**: the connecting user's own automations step by step — or names only — a dummy automation the user can open but was never pointed at, the connected-app list, past run and task data (the cargo, not just the route), and a **control Zap** held in a separate account that was never connected at all. The filmed question is whether the reach is the one automation in the prompt, or the whole account — configurations, credentials' destinations, and history included.
- The write reach is the part this issue is about. Trigger an automation, edit a step, enable a disabled one, disable a live one — each filmed in Teardown #47 with the approval step, or its absence, on camera. Three of those writes are visible. The fourth kind is not: an edit does not announce itself to the people whose data the automation moves, and an enable sets standing instructions running under stored credentials for every app in the chain, indefinitely, under the connecting user's name — unless the film shows otherwise.
- There is no dataset shortcut for this one. Zapier / Make → Claude is **not** one of the 13 connectors in the published documentation audit, so no documentation score, factor code or band carries over — and nothing carries over from Teardown #19 either, which tested the same automation account from a different AI side. Teardown #47's scorecard is blank until the film.

That is a pattern, not a finding about a named product. No vendor score is published here, and Teardown #47 is not filmed yet — its scores are blank until the film. Check the pattern in your own estate first — the fix is in Section 5.

## 2. Field Note — The Automation Nobody Watches

Every estate has them. A Zap built in 2021 to copy new form submissions into a spreadsheet. A scenario that moves new customers from the CRM into the mailing tool, the billing tool and a Slack channel. A webhook that posts every support ticket above a severity into a spreadsheet someone exported once and never opened again. They were built by a person solving a problem that week, approved — if the word even applies — by that same person clicking "turn on", and they have run, faithfully, through reorganisations, migrations and offboardings ever since. Ask who owns them now and the honest answer, in most estates, is nobody. The builder left. The account is a shared login, or a personal account on a company card. The automations are not in the asset register, not in the data inventory, and not in anyone's review cycle, because they were never classified as what they are: connectors, holding delegated access to two or more systems each, moving personal data between them under stored credentials, on standing instructions.

That is the first half of the note: the automation layer is the connector behind the connectors. Every other issue of this newsletter has examined a grant between a person and a system. The automation account is a rack of grants between systems, pre-approved, self-executing, and — unlike a person with a login — never prompted to re-authenticate, never subject to a leaver's process, never bored, never careful. It copies the new-hire list to the tool with the weakest access controls in the chain because that was the tool the builder liked. It emails the day's orders, with names and addresses, to a personal inbox "for backup". None of this is hypothetical malice; it is ordinary helpfulness, fossilised. The data protection review, where one exists, reviewed the CRM and the spreadsheet as systems. Nobody reviewed the pipe, and the pipe is where the data actually travels.

The second half is what changes when an AI assistant connects to that account — the pattern Teardown #47 films. Until now, the automation layer's obscurity was also its protection: to misuse it, a person had to know it existed, log in, and read configurations one screen at a time. An AI connector collapses that. The whole account — every workflow, every connected app, every field mapping, and the run history showing what has actually moved — becomes searchable and summarisable in one conversation, and the writes are worse than the reads: an agent that can edit a step can re-point every future run, and an agent that can enable a dormant Zap can wake a workflow nobody remembers, connected to apps nobody has reviewed since, moving data nobody has classified. The fossil starts moving again, at machine speed, under a live grant.

None of this requires a hostile actor. It requires an ordinary estate, where automations were treated as plumbing instead of as delegated access. Teardown #47 tests the governed version of this pattern — Zapier / Make → Claude on a test account, dummy Zaps, dummy apps and dummy data only, with the read reach, the enable/edit tests and the run-history trail filmed. It is not filmed yet, and this note claims no result from it. The leader's version needs no film: pick your own automation account and ask three questions in the room. Can anyone list every active automation, what it moves, and between which systems? Does every one of them have a named owner who is still in the building? And if an AI assistant connected to that account tomorrow, what exactly — configurations, run history, enable rights — would it hold? If the room cannot answer the first two, the third has already been decided by default.

The lesson for leaders: an automation is a connector with standing instructions and your data in motion. It deserves what every other connector in this newsletter gets — an inventory entry, an owner, a review, and a scope. Section 3 gives the rules.

## 3. Harness — Automation governance: inventory them as connectors, own them, and fence the AI that edits them

Issue #5's field note was two doors, one vault: the same CRM connected twice — once by a person, once by an automation — reviewed as two separate small risks and never as a pair, so the combined reach into the vault was nobody's number. The automation layer is that pattern at estate scale: every Zap is a second door into each app it touches, opened under stored credentials, and the AI connector is a door into all the doors. This section is the governance that follows from pricing it that way. Three rules, all of which belong in the approval record, not in a prompt.

**Rule 1 — Automations are inventoried as connectors, in the same register, keyed by data source.** Issue #5's Monday fix was one registry, keyed by data source, so a pair of grants into the same store is reviewed as a pair. The automation account joins that registry as what it is: each active automation gets a line naming its trigger, its steps, the apps at both ends, the data classes it moves (Issue #17's classes apply to the cargo, not just the configuration), and the credentials it runs under. An automation that cannot be described in that line — because nobody knows what it does — is not a mystery to be tolerated; it is an unknown to be recorded as an unknown, with an owner and a date, the same discipline Issue #39 applied to the attendee-list question. Disabled and draft automations are inventoried too: a disabled Zap is a pre-built, pre-credentialed workflow one click — or one agent action — from live.

**Rule 2 — Every automation has a named owner and a review date, or it is switched off.** Ownership here is a person, in post, whose job includes answering for what the automation moves. The review is the connector review, run on a cycle: is this workflow still needed, does it still move only what it should, are the apps at both ends still approved, do the stored credentials still belong to accounts the estate controls? The leaver's process — Issue #16's revoke list — extends to the automation account explicitly: when a builder leaves, their automations transfer to a named owner or stop, and their personal automation accounts are checked for company workflows, the automation form of Issue #23's personal-account problem. The default for an orphaned automation is off. A workflow nobody will own is a workflow nobody can say should be running, and "it has always run" is not an approval.

**Rule 3 — The AI-editing-automations rule: an agent may read the wiring only as approved, and may change it only behind a human step.** This is the rule this issue exists to state. Connecting an AI assistant to the automation account is approved in two separate grants, the way Issue #39 split calendar write rights from invite rights. *Read* is granted automation by automation or by named class — never as "the account" — and whether run history (the cargo record) is inside the read is stated in the approval in so many words. *Write* — trigger, edit, enable, disable — is a second decision with a harder default: an agent may draft an edit and describe an enable, and a named human performs or approves both in the platform, because an edit re-points every future run and an enable is a go-live, and go-lives are human decisions everywhere else in the estate. Where the platform cannot separate the agent's draft from its execution, that limitation goes in the approval record and the write tier drops to what the record can defend — Issue #36's discipline, applied to the wiring. And the log position is tested, not assumed: can the estate show, afterwards, which session edited which step and which session enabled which Zap? That is Issue #21's one-hour reconstruction test and Issue #25's evidence rule, applied to the automation layer; if the platform's history names only the account owner for the agent's changes, the approval record says so, and the agent's rights are set to what that record can support.

**The agent-specific line.** None of these rules is a setting on the automation platform. They live in the approval record — the register line, the owner's name, the review date, the read/write split for AI — which is precisely why they get missed: the automation was never in the room when connector governance was designed, because it was filed under plumbing. Price the automation account as the rack of standing grants it is, and the AI connection to it as a grant over all of them, and the governance writes itself.

## 5. The Monday Fix — inventory one automation account as connectors

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — one automation account, and the three rules from Section 3:

1. Pick **one automation account** — the oldest, or the one whose builder has left, not the tidiest. Export or list every automation in it, active, disabled and draft. For each, write the register line: trigger, steps, apps at both ends, data classes moved, credentials used. If an automation's purpose cannot be stated, record it as an unknown with today's date — that is the first finding, and it goes in the register as one (Issue #25's rule: evidence or it didn't happen).
2. **Name the owners.** Beside each line, write the owner — a person, in post. Every automation with no owner gets a disposition in the meeting, not after it: transfer to a named owner with a review date, or switch it off. Check the leaver overlap explicitly: any automation built by someone who has left is owned or off by the end of the session, and any company workflow found running in a personal automation account is migrated or recorded as accepted risk with an expiry.
3. **Write the AI line before anyone connects one.** Add the automation account to the connector register as a grant over all its lines, and write the two decisions into its approval record: which automations an AI assistant may read (and whether run history is included), and the write rule — draft and describe freely, edit and enable only behind a named human in the platform. Walk it once today: confirm whether your platform's history would show an AI session's edit as the agent or as the account owner, and file the answer.
4. **Set the review trigger.** Write the cycle into the register: the account is re-reviewed quarterly alongside the connector register (Issue #34's sweep), and three events reopen the approval immediately — a new automation created, a new app connected to the account, and any AI assistant connected to it. File the page — register lines, owners, the AI read/write decisions, the log answer — with the connector approvals. Then book the same forty-five minutes for the next automation account. One account a week is slow, and it is also how an estate's wiring actually gets governed.

Score the connector before you trust it — and inventory the connectors behind the connectors, because the Zap nobody watches is still a standing instruction moving your data, and the day an AI assistant connects to that account, every forgotten workflow in it becomes part of the grant. This Monday, one automation account's inventory is the fix.
