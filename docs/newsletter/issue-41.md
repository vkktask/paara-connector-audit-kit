# The Connector Audit — Issue #41

**Score the connector before you trust it.**

For CIOs, CISOs, CPOs, risk officers and the people who advise them. One connector pattern, one field note, one harness section, one regulator or industry-body update, and one Monday fix — every week.

**Issue #41 headline: The Customer's Number — a messaging connector does not read your files or your calendar, it reads and writes to your customers, on the number they trust, in the channel they treat as personal: their data, their consent and their opt-out, in an AI's hands. Plus customer-messaging rules for agents — an opt-out that stops the agent and not just the campaign, customer-facing templates approved as artefacts rather than prompts, and a staffed human takeover out of the bot — and the FTC Impersonation Rule ANPRM, reported strictly as an open consultation with comments due 30 November 2026.**

## 1. The Connector Pattern — the messaging layer, reached from a second AI side

This week's pattern to check in your own estate:

Teardown #26 tested WhatsApp → a ChatGPT-style AI assistant. Teardown #48 tests **WhatsApp → a Claude-style AI assistant**: the same messaging store — one-to-one chats, group chats, a customer thread on the business number, media, and a control chat the connecting account was never part of — reached from a different AI assistant. The pair is the pattern. The chats do not change, the route does, and the route is where the risk lives. A second AI side means a second grant over the most candid store in the estate, a second approval surface, and a second log story.

- The read reach is conversation, not documents. Tested in Teardown #48 on **test numbers and accounts the tester owns, with dummy chats only**: one-to-one chats between the tester's own dummy accounts, a **group chat** whose members are all dummy accounts, a dummy **customer thread** on the test business number, dummy media where the setup allows, and a **control chat** between two dummy accounts the connecting account was never a member of. The filmed question is whether the reach is the chat in the prompt, or the whole account — groups, customer threads and history included — and whether the control chat stays out of reach.
- The write reach is the part this issue is about. Send a message to a chat, send one to a group, reply inside the customer thread on the business number — each filmed in Teardown #48 with the approval step, or its absence, on camera, and with the question the other teardowns do not have to ask in this form: **whose name and number do the messages carry?** A message sent from the business number arrives as the business. A reply in a customer thread is read by the customer as the business answering, in the channel the customer treats as personal and immediate — whether a person wrote it or not.
- There is no dataset shortcut for this one. WhatsApp → a Claude-style assistant is **not** one of the 13 connectors in the published documentation audit, so no documentation score, factor code or band carries over — and nothing carries over from Teardown #26 either, which tested the same messaging store from a different AI side. Teardown #48's scorecard is blank until the film.

That is a pattern, not a finding about a named product. No vendor score is published here, and Teardown #48 is not filmed yet — its scores are blank until the film. Check the pattern in your own estate first — the fix is in Section 5.

## 2. Field Note — The Customer's Number

Every other store in this series belongs to the organisation. The mailbox is the company's. The calendar is the company's. The file store, the CRM, the automation account — the company's, holding other people's data, but reached through systems the company runs. The business messaging number is different in one respect that changes everything downstream: it lives in the customer's pocket. The customer saved it, or did not save it and still recognises it, because it is the number they message when something is wrong, when an order is late, when they need a person. It is the most trusted address the business owns, precisely because it does not feel like an address at all. It feels like a conversation.

That is what a messaging connector touches when it touches customers directly. Not a record about the customer — the customer. Their thread holds what they actually wrote: the complaint in their own words, the photo of the damaged delivery, the voice note, the address they typed at midnight, the health detail or the money worry they would never have put in a form, because a chat does not feel like a form. When an AI assistant is connected to that number, all of that sits inside its read reach — and the write reach is the sharper half. An agent that replies in the customer thread does not reply as software. It replies as the business, under the business's name and number, into the one channel where the customer cannot tell the difference and has no reason to try.

Now price the three things that travel with that thread. **The customer's data**: everything in the thread, and — if the read reach is account-wide, which is exactly what Teardown #48 films — everything in every other thread on the number, summarisable in one conversation the customer will never see. **The customer's consent**: the customer agreed, at most, to message the business. They did not agree to be read by an assistant, summarised by an assistant, or answered by one, and the approval that connected the assistant was given by the business, not by them — Issue #19's bystander problem, except the bystander here is not a colleague in a group chat. It is a customer, with a customer's rights and a customer's expectations. **The customer's opt-out**: the customer who replies STOP, or "do not message me again", has exercised the one control the channel gives them. Whether that control binds the agent — or only the campaign tool the business used before the agent arrived — is a design question most estates have never asked, because until now there was nothing on the number except people and a sending tool.

Teardown #48 tests the governed version of this pattern — WhatsApp → a Claude-style AI assistant on test numbers the tester owns, dummy chats, a dummy customer thread and dummy data only, with the read reach, the three send tests and the log trail filmed, including whether reads are logged anywhere at all. It is not filmed yet, and this note claims no result from it. The leader's version needs no film: pick up your own business number's thread list and ask three questions in the room. Can anyone say which AI assistants, summarises or agents can currently read those threads? When the agent replies, does anything — anywhere the customer can see — say so? And if a customer opts out tonight, what exactly stops: the campaign, the tool, or every route by which an agent could still message them? If the room cannot answer the third question in one sentence, the opt-out is a promise the estate has not wired.

The lesson for leaders: a messaging connector is not a support feature. It is delegated access to the customer's side of the conversation — their words, their consent, their one control — exercised under your name and number. It deserves to be governed as customer contact, not as plumbing. Section 3 gives the rules.

## 3. Harness — Customer-messaging rules for agents: the opt-out binds the agent, templates are artefacts, and there is a staffed way out

Issue #19 set the bystander tests for connected stores: the **reach test** (whose data is actually inside the grant), the **notice test** (how would a bystander find out), and the **refusal test** (what happens if a bystander objects — with remedy: a working route that honours the objection, not just records it). The customer thread is where those three tests stop being abstract. The bystander is a customer, the store is a conversation the customer is still having, and refusal has a name the customer already knows: the opt-out. This section is Issue #19's tests written as operating rules for any agent that can read or write customer messaging. Three rules, all of which belong in the approval record, not in a prompt.

**Rule 1 — Opt-in and opt-out are honoured by the agent, not just by the campaign.** The approval record states, in so many words, which customers the agent may message at all (the opt-in position: who agreed to be contacted, through which route, for what) and how an opt-out reaches the agent. The standard is simple to state and routinely failed in practice: **an opt-out stops the agent, not just the campaign.** If a customer opts out, the suppression applies to every route that can message them from that number — the campaign tool, the support flow, and the connected assistant — because the customer did not opt out of a tool. They opted out of the business messaging them. The harness test is mechanical: an opt-out recorded in any one system must be visible to the agent before its next send, and the agent's send path must check it as configuration, not remember it as an instruction. An opt-out that lives in a prompt, or in a list the agent was told about last week, is not a control. Where the platform cannot enforce suppression on the agent's route, that limitation goes in the approval record and the agent's write rights drop to what the record can defend — the same discipline Issue #40 applied to automation writes. This is Issue #19's refusal test with its remedy attached: refusal that does not stop the sends is notice wearing consent's clothes.

**Rule 2 — Customer-facing templates are approved as artefacts, not prompts.** What an agent may say to a customer is not a style instruction. The messages an agent sends from the business number — the order update, the appointment reminder, the support reply, the re-engagement nudge — are publications under the business's name, and they are approved the way Issue #32's harness approved outward-facing actions: a named human sees the **actual artefact** — the template text as the customer will receive it, with its merge fields and its fallback wording — before it goes live, and the approved version is the version on file. Three consequences follow. First, a template change is a re-approval: an agent that can reword its own customer messages holds a publishing right nobody granted, so template text lives outside the agent's write reach. Second, the approval covers the fields, not just the prose: a template that inserts the customer's name, order detail or balance is a data decision as well as a wording decision, and the approver signs both. Third, free-form agent replies to customers are a separate, harder grant from template sends — an agent approved to send three approved templates has not been approved to improvise, and the approval record says which of the two it holds. This is Issue #19's notice test made concrete: the customer cannot be told an agent is writing to them if the business itself has never read what the agent writes.

**Rule 3 — Human takeover is a staffed route out of the bot, not a menu option.** Every customer thread an agent can write in has a named route to a person: the customer can ask for a human — in words, not only by pressing the right option — and the agent's job at that moment is to hand over, not to retain. The harness requirements are the ones Issue #28 set for escalation generally, priced for a customer on the other end: the takeover is **staffed** (a rota with names and hours, not a mailbox nobody watches), the handover is a **package** (the thread, what the agent has already said and done in it, and the customer's request, arriving together — the customer does not re-explain themselves to the human as the price of escaping the bot), and the agent **stops** in that thread once takeover is requested, rather than continuing to reply in parallel under the same number. The takeover route is tested before go-live and on a cycle afterwards: a test message asking for a human, timed, with the result filed — Issue #25's evidence rule applied to the exit door. A takeover option that routes to an unstaffed queue is Issue #19's refusal test failed a second time: the form of a remedy without the substance.

**The agent-specific line.** None of these rules is a setting on the messaging platform. They live in the approval record — the opt-in position, the suppression path, the approved template versions, the takeover rota — which is precisely why they get missed: the connector was approved as a support tool, and support tools are not usually asked who approved their words, whether their opt-outs bind, or who answers when a customer asks for a person. Price the messaging connector as what it is — the business's voice, in the customer's pocket, at machine speed — and the governance writes itself.

## 4. Regulator / Industry-Body Update — FTC Impersonation Rule ANPRM: an open consultation, comments due 30 November 2026

This week's update is an open consultation, reported at exactly that status — the same consultation Issue #19 flagged, revisited because its deadline is now the near one on the calendar.

The industry-bodies scan published with this newsletter's work on 16 November 2026 ranked the **US Federal Trade Commission's Impersonation Rule advance notice of proposed rulemaking (ANPRM)** fourth of eleven routes: a **virtual** route — comments are submitted in writing, no travel or attendance involved — with **comments due 30 November 2026**. The scan rated the fit **tangential** for a connector-governance practice, and that rating stands: this is flagged as an open consultation a reader may choose to answer, at that honest distance.

That is the complete, current status: **a consultation identified as open, with a 30 November 2026 comment deadline.** No submission is claimed here — nothing has been sent to the FTC under this newsletter's work, and anything sent will be reported only when it exists, at the same precision as the other entries in this series.

Why does an impersonation consultation belong in an issue about customer messaging? Because this week's pattern is the consultation's question, running inside the estate. The impersonation question regulators are asking — when a message, a voice or a persona is not what it appears to be, who is responsible, and what must be disclosed — is the same question Section 1 raises about the business number: an AI-sent reply arrives looking exactly like the business's own, under the business's name and number, to a customer who has no way to see the difference. The customer reading an agent's reply and the consumer facing an impersonation are in the same position: dealing with a message whose true author they cannot see. A practitioner comment that brings evidence from the connector layer — how these connections actually grant send-as rights on a customer-facing number, and what disclosure the platforms do and do not support — is the kind of input an ANPRM exists to collect. Tangential fit, real seam. The deadline is 30 November 2026.

## 5. The Monday Fix — test the opt-out on your own business number

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — one business messaging number, and the three rules from Section 3:

1. Pick **one customer-facing messaging number** — the support or sales number customers actually write to, not the quietest one. List every route that can send from it: the campaign tool, the support platform, and every connected AI assistant or agent. If a route is discovered in this exercise that nobody in the room knew could send, that is the first finding, and it goes in the connector register as one.
2. **Run the opt-out test, live.** From a test phone, message the number and opt out the way a customer would — reply STOP, or use the business's stated opt-out wording. Then check each sending route from Step 1: is the test number suppressed in all of them, including the agent's? Attempt one agent send to the test number through the normal workflow and record whether it is blocked, warned, or sent. File the result with the date — evidence, not recollection (Issue #25's rule). If the agent's route is not suppressed, the disposition is written in the meeting: wire the suppression, or record the agent's write rights as reduced until it is.
3. **Approve one template as an artefact.** Take the customer message the agent sends most often. Print the exact text as the customer receives it — merge fields filled with dummy values — and have the named approver sign that version, with its fields, into the approval record. Note next to it which grant the agent holds: approved templates only, or free-form replies as well. If nobody can produce the exact current wording, the agent has been publishing unapproved text, and that goes in the record too.
4. **Test the way out.** From the test phone, ask for a human, in plain words. Time how long until a person — a named person, on the rota — holds the thread, and check what arrived with the handover: the thread, the agent's earlier messages, the request. Confirm the agent stopped replying in that thread once takeover was requested. Write the rota's name and hours into the approval record, or record the gap as accepted risk with an expiry and an owner.

Score the connector before you trust it — and when the connector speaks to customers, score the words, the opt-out and the exit as part of the connector, because the customer cannot tell your agent from you, and should never have to. This Monday, one number's opt-out, tested live, is the fix.
