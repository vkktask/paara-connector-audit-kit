# The Connector Audit — Issue #36

**Score the connector before you trust it.**

For CIOs, CISOs, CPOs, risk officers and the people who advise them. One connector pattern, one field note, one harness section, one regulator or industry-body update, and one Monday fix — every week.

**Issue #36 headline: The Shared Mailbox, Again — Issue #15 showed how one person's account quietly inherits every shared mailbox it can open; this sequel asks the harder questions underneath: whose identity is acting, whose consent was ever given, and whose log would tell the story afterwards. Plus mailbox and delegation rules for agents — delegate access versus full access, send-as versus send-on-behalf, and the record of who really acted — and a short-form book proposal inquiry to the IET: an inquiry sent, awaiting a reply, not a contract.**

## 1. The Connector Pattern — the mailbox estate, reached from a second AI side

This week's pattern to check in your own estate:

Teardown #22 tested Outlook / Microsoft 365 Mail → ChatGPT. Teardown #43 tests **Outlook / Microsoft 365 Mail → Claude**: the same mail platform, the same tenant model, reached from a different AI assistant. The store does not change, the route does, and the route is where the risk lives. A second AI side means a second grant over the same mailboxes, a second approval surface, and a second log story.

- The read reach is the mailbox estate as the account actually holds it. A work account rarely opens one mailbox. It opens its own — years of threads, contracts, HR conversations, customer escalations, attachments, drafts and deleted items — plus the shared and delegated mailboxes accumulated over a career: support, finance, HR, the team aliases that outlive every reorganisation. Tested in Teardown #43 on a **test tenant with dummy mailboxes only**: the connecting user's own mailbox folder by folder, a **shared mailbox** the user was granted access to but was never pointed at, and a **control mailbox** never shared with or delegated to the connecting user. The filmed question is whether the reach is the one mailbox in the conversation, or every mailbox the account can open.
- The write reach is wider than the summarising story that sold it. Draft, send as the user, reply, delete — and the write this teardown adds to the mail series: **create a rule**, a write that keeps acting after the session ends, on mail nobody has seen yet. Where the user holds send-as or send-on-behalf rights on a shared mailbox, the write reach also includes sending under the team's name. Each write is filmed in Teardown #43 with the approval step, or its absence, on camera.
- There is no dataset shortcut for this one. Outlook / Microsoft 365 Mail → Claude is **not** one of the 13 connectors in the published documentation audit, so no documentation score, factor code or band carries over — and nothing carries over from Teardown #22 either, which tested the same mail platform from a different AI side. Teardown #43's scorecard is blank until the film.

That is a pattern, not a finding about a named product. No vendor score is published here, and Teardown #43 is not filmed yet — its scores are blank until the film. Check the pattern in your own estate first — the fix is in Section 5.

## 2. Field Note — The Shared Mailbox, Again

Issue #15 made the reach argument: connect an AI assistant to one person's account and it quietly inherits every shared and team mailbox that account can open — a whole function's correspondence, granted in one click, assessed by nobody. That argument has had time to settle. This sequel is about the three questions that sit underneath it, because they are the ones that decide what happens after something goes wrong.

**Whose identity is acting?** A shared mailbox exists precisely because the work is not one person's. When a human opens it, the platform still knows which human it was — the mailbox is shared, the login is not. A connector collapses that distinction. The session runs under the connecting person's grant, reads as that person, sends as that person — or, where send-as rights exist, sends *as the mailbox itself*, under the team's name, with no person attached at all. Months later, a message exists in Sent Items, a customer acted on it, and the question "who sent this?" has three possible answers — the person, the person's AI session, the mailbox acting through the person's rights — and the record may not be able to separate them. Identity was the first factor in the PAARA taxonomy for a reason: every other question — reach, logging, revocation — is asked about an identity, and a shared mailbox is where estates discover they never decided which identity the agent has.

**Whose consent was ever given?** The person who clicked connect consented — to something; the consent screen named an account, not the mailboxes that account can open. The shared mailbox's other users never saw a screen. The customers, employees and suppliers whose correspondence fills that mailbox never saw one either. Consent in a shared mailbox was always collective and implicit — colleagues accept that teammates can read the queue because the queue is the job. An AI session is not a teammate. It reads at machine speed, it summarises outside the mailbox, and it can be hijacked by text inside the mail it reads — Teardown #43 plants exactly that, a hidden instruction inside a dummy message, with copies in the shared mailbox and in a subject line. The bystander question from Issue #19 applies to mail with full force: the people inside the data are affected parties, and nobody asked them.

**Whose log tells the story?** Microsoft 365 keeps a message trace and a tenant audit log — real logs, better than most connectors offer. But the log was designed around human actors and message events: sends, deletes, rule changes. The reads that matter most in a connector incident — the mailbox searched, the thread summarised, the attachment opened by a session that then did something elsewhere — are the events least likely to be in it. And where an entry does exist, it names the account, not the session: the person's name stands in the record for work the person's agent did. That is not a logging failure to blame a vendor for. It is a design assumption — one actor per account — that connectors break by construction.

Teardown #43 tests this pattern on Outlook / Microsoft 365 Mail → Claude: mailbox read reach with shared mailboxes and a control mailbox, write actions — send, draft, delete, rules — a hidden instruction planted in a dummy email, and the message-trace and audit-log trail behind it all. It is filmed on a test tenant with dummy mailboxes only, it is not filmed yet, and this note claims no result from it. The pattern does not need a result: open your own estate's shared mailboxes, list who can open each one, ask which of those people has connected an AI tool to their account, and then ask the three questions above. The answers are the finding, and they are already available to you.

The lesson for leaders: a shared mailbox connected to AI is not a mailbox with a feature switched on. It is a collective record placed inside one person's grant, under an identity nobody chose, on consent nobody collected, with a log that will name the wrong actor. Section 3 gives the rules that fix the identity part — the part that can actually be fixed by decision.

## 3. Harness — Mailbox and delegation rules for agents

Issue #15's harness section set out the three identity patterns every agent runs under — its own identity, a borrowed user identity, a shared service account. This section is the mail-specific working of that lesson: the delegation rules to write into the approval record before any mail connector goes live, because mail is where borrowed identity does the most quiet damage.

**Rule 1 — Delegate access is a scope, not a courtesy; full access is a different decision.** Mail platforms distinguish, in their own permission models, between levels of access to another mailbox: the ability to read and manage mail in it, the ability to send from it, and full access that treats the mailbox as effectively your own. Human delegation grants grew casually because a human delegate is slow, visible and socially accountable. An agent holding the same grant is none of those. The rule: the connector approval names, mailbox by mailbox, which level of access the agent's grant actually rides on, and any mailbox where the connecting user holds more than the use case needs is either re-scoped for the human first or written into the approval as accepted risk with a named owner. "It came with the account" — Issue #15's line — is not a delegation decision.

**Rule 2 — Send-as and send-on-behalf are different signatures; choose one, in writing.** Sending *on behalf of* a mailbox names both parties: the mailbox, and the person who acted. Sending *as* the mailbox names only the mailbox — the message is, on its face, the team's, and no reader can tell a person was involved at all. For human delegates the difference is etiquette. For an agent it is the whole accountability story: a send-as message composed and sent by an AI session carries the team's authority with no human in the visible record. The rule: agent sessions send on behalf, never as — the human in the loop is named in the message itself — or they do not send from shared mailboxes at all. Where the platform cannot enforce that distinction for the connector's grant, the approval record says so, and shared-mailbox sending drops off the approved use case.

**Rule 3 — The record must say who really acted, or the approval record must admit it cannot.** After any agent session that touched mail, the estate needs to be able to answer: which messages were read, drafted, sent, deleted or ruled on by the session, under which grant, in which mailbox — separately from the mailbox owner's own actions. Where the audit log does separate connector activity from the user's, that separation is verified in a drill, the way Issue #10 drills the kill switch and Issue #21 drills reconstruction: run a session on a test mailbox, then rebuild it from the log alone, in the room, before go-live. Where the log cannot separate them — where the agent's send is indistinguishable from the person's — the gap is written into the approval record in those words, and the connector's write tier drops accordingly: an agent whose sends cannot be told apart from a human's does not get send rights on shared mailboxes. This is Issue #35's third gate, applied to mail: the log names the agent, or the rights shrink to what the log can support.

**Rule 4 — Rules are writes that outlive the session; approve them like standing access.** A forwarding or filing rule created in a session keeps acting on future mail indefinitely, including mail the session never saw. The approval therefore treats rule creation as its own write class: either the connector cannot create rules at all, or every rule it creates is listed in the mailbox's rule inventory within a day, with an owner and an expiry — the same discipline Issue #31 applies to shared links, because a rule and a link are the same shape: a grant nobody filed, still working after everyone forgot it.

**The agent-specific line.** None of these rules is a setting on the connector. They live in the tenant's delegation model and the approval record — which is precisely why they get missed: the connector review looked at scopes, and the delegation decisions lived somewhere else. Price the mail grant against the delegation model as it actually stands, the way Issue #34 prices a grant against the tenant as it actually stands.

## 4. Regulator / Industry-Body Update — IET short-form book proposal: a pre-submission inquiry sent, awaiting a reply

This week's update is an inquiry, reported at exactly that status — which is earlier than a submission, and this section will not dress it up as one.

**On 16 November 2026, a pre-submission inquiry about a short-form book proposal was sent to the Institution of Engineering and Technology (IET), addressed to author_support@theiet.org.** A pre-submission inquiry is what its name says: a letter before a proposal, asking the publisher whether the proposed short-form book — practitioner method on governing AI connectors and agents, in the line of the work this newsletter documents — is the kind of book its programme publishes, and on what terms, before a formal proposal is prepared and sent.

The status, stated at its actual precision: **inquiry sent on 16 November 2026; reply awaited.** No proposal has been formally submitted, no contract exists, no terms have been offered, and nothing about this book is agreed. It is reported here for the same reason this section reports submissions as submitted-not-accepted: the distance between "we wrote to a publisher" and "we have a book deal" is exactly the distance that gets quietly erased in bios and press releases, and a newsletter about evidence standards does not erase it in its own pages. If a reply arrives, it will be reported here as a new fact with a date on it — including if the answer is no.

Why the IET, and why short-form? Because the audience for connector governance is practising engineers and the leaders who sign their approvals, and a short-form practitioner title is the format that reaches them while the method is still current — the same reason the kit, the dataset and the training are open source now rather than waiting for a longer book cycle. The inquiry-first route is deliberate: it asks the publisher to examine the fit and the terms before either side commits to a manuscript.

## 5. The Monday Fix — write one mailbox's delegation rules

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — one shared mailbox a connector can already reach, three rules written down:

1. Pick **one shared mailbox that a connected account can open** — the busiest team mailbox is the honest start. From the tenant's own admin views, not from memory, list: who can open it, at what level (read/manage, send-on-behalf, send-as, full access), and which of those people has an AI connector on their account. Record the list with a date on it — Issue #25's rule: evidence or it didn't happen.
2. **Write the three decisions into the connector's approval record:** the delegation level the agent's grant is approved to ride on for this mailbox (Rule 1); the signature rule — on behalf, never as, or no sending from this mailbox at all (Rule 2); and the rule-creation position — not permitted, or inventoried within a day with an owner and an expiry (Rule 4). Each decision gets a named owner, not a team name.
3. **Test the record, don't trust it:** take one recent day's activity in this mailbox and rebuild it from the audit log alone — sends, deletes, rule changes, and whose name each carries. Whatever the log cannot attribute — above all, anything an agent session did that reads as the owner's own work — write into the approval record as a named gap, and drop the connector's write rights on this mailbox to what the log can support until the gap closes (Rule 3).
4. **File the page** — the access list, the three decisions, the log test and its gaps — with the mailbox's connector approval, and set the review trigger from Issue #15: any change to this mailbox's access list re-opens the approval. Shared-mailbox access changes quietly and often; the approval has to hear about it.

Score the connector before you trust it — and write the delegation rules before the agent inherits them, because a shared mailbox under one person's grant is everyone's correspondence at one click's distance. This Monday, the mailbox page is the fix.
