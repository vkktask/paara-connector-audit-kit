# The Connector Audit — Issue #39

**Score the connector before you trust it.**

For CIOs, CISOs, CPOs, risk officers and the people who advise them. One connector pattern, one field note, one harness section, one regulator or industry-body update, and one Monday fix — every week.

**Issue #39 headline: The Invite List — an agent connected to your calendar does not just read your time, it reads and changes who meets whom: every attendee list is a map of your real relationships, and every invitation it sends goes out in your name to people who never agreed to deal with an agent. Plus calendar rules for agents — which calendars, what invite rights, and how external attendees are handled — and the ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 standards route via INCITS/AI: virtual and hybrid, rolling, and reported here as a route, not a membership.**

## 1. The Connector Pattern — the calendar store, reached from a second AI side

This week's pattern to check in your own estate:

Teardown #25 tested Google Calendar → ChatGPT. Teardown #46 tests **Google Calendar → Claude**: the same calendar store — primary and secondary calendars, calendars shared in by colleagues, private events, descriptions, attachments and attendee lists — reached from a different AI assistant. The pair is the pattern. The store does not change, the route does, and the route is where the risk lives. A second AI side means a second grant over the same diary, a second approval surface, and a second log story.

- The read reach is the diary as the account actually holds it. Tested in Teardown #46 on a **test account with dummy calendars and dummy events only**: the connecting user's own calendars surface by surface, a **shared calendar** the user can open but was never pointed at, **private events** — full contents, or a busy block only — and a **control calendar** owned by a second account that was never shared at all. The filmed question is whether the reach is the one event in the prompt, or every calendar the account can open — and whether the attendee lists, with their email addresses, come back as part of the read.
- The write reach is the part this issue is about. Create an event, edit one, delete one, and **invite other people** — each filmed in Teardown #46 with the approval step, or its absence, on camera. Three of those writes stay inside the account. The fourth leaves the building: an invitation lands in someone else's inbox and on someone else's calendar, under the connecting user's name, and nothing in it says an AI session sent it — unless the film shows otherwise.
- There is no dataset shortcut for this one. Google Calendar → Claude is **not** one of the 13 connectors in the published documentation audit, so no documentation score, factor code or band carries over — and nothing carries over from Teardown #25 either, which tested the same calendar store from a different AI side. Teardown #46's scorecard is blank until the film.

That is a pattern, not a finding about a named product. No vendor score is published here, and Teardown #46 is not filmed yet — its scores are blank until the film. Check the pattern in your own estate first — the fix is in Section 5.

## 2. Field Note — The Invite List

Issue #18 called the calendar a confession: read whole, it leaks strategy — who meets whom, when, about what. That note was about the diary as a document. This one is about the diary as a network, and about the moment the agent stops reading it and starts writing to the people in it.

Start with the attendee list, because it is the part of a calendar nobody classifies. An event title can be vague — "Sync", "Call", "1:1" — and the description can be empty. The attendee list cannot be vague: it names the people, with their email addresses, in a combination that is itself the information. The candidate plus the hiring manager plus the external recruiter is an interview loop, whatever the event is called. The founder plus the investor plus the lawyer is a round, or an exit. The employee plus HR plus an employment counsel is a process nobody announced. Read one calendar and you have a schedule. Read the attendee lists across a calendar and you have the organisation's real relationship map — who actually works with whom, who is being courted, who is being managed out — drawn by the people themselves, kept current by the tool they trust most, and almost never listed in any data inventory as what it is: relationship data, about named third parties, most of whom never consented to be mapped.

Then the write side, which is where the invite list stops being read and starts being used. An agent with calendar write access does not merely edit records; it corresponds. It invites. An invitation is a message — from you, in your name, arriving in someone else's inbox with the full social weight of a request from a person. When an agent sends it, three things the recipient assumes are all false at once: that you chose the time, that you chose them, and that a human will notice if they reply. The recipient who accepts rearranges their week around a commitment no person made. The recipient who declines, or asks why, is writing back to a mailbox whose owner may never see the exchange the agent started. And the deletion is the mirror image: an event removed by an agent disappears from the organiser's diary with a click of housekeeping, while the attendees' copies, travel and preparation hang off a meeting that, in the only diary anyone checked, no longer exists.

None of this requires a hostile actor. It requires an ordinary assistant doing ordinary diary work at machine speed, under a grant that was reviewed — if it was reviewed — as a scheduling convenience. The approval conversation was about the account holder's time. The attendee list is everybody else's data, and the invitation is everybody else's inbox, and neither was in the room.

Teardown #46 tests the governed version of this pattern — Google Calendar → Claude on a test account, dummy calendars, dummy events and dummy attendee addresses that cannot reach a real person, with the read reach, the invite test and the audit trail filmed. It is not filmed yet, and this note claims no result from it. The leader's version needs no film: pick one calendar connection in your own estate and ask three questions in the room. Which calendars can it read — including the ones shared in, that nobody thinks of as "connected"? Are attendee lists and their addresses inside that read, and is that written down anywhere? And can it invite — send commitments in a person's name to people outside — and if it can, who approved that, in those words? If the room answers the first two with a shrug and has never asked the third, the invite list is ungoverned in the only sense that matters.

The lesson for leaders: a calendar connector is delegated access to a relationship map, plus a pen that writes to everyone on it. Section 3 gives the rules: which calendars, what invite rights, and how the other people on the invite are treated.

## 3. Harness — Calendar rules for agents

Issue #29 set the bystander rule for scheduling tools: the other party to a booking never consented, so consent and notice have to be designed for them. This section is the calendar version, written for the general case — any agent connected to any diary in the estate. Four rules, all of which belong in the approval record, not in a prompt.

**Rule 1 — Which calendars is a list, not a default.** The approval names the calendars in scope, one by one: the primary work calendar, yes; the personal calendar synced into the same account, decided explicitly, not inherited; shared-in calendars — a team's absence calendar, a manager's diary, a project calendar — each named, because "every calendar the account can open" is not a scope, it is an accident of sharing settings other people chose for other reasons. Calendars shared in after the approval are out of scope until the approval is amended — a sharing change is a connector change, the same re-approval trigger Issue #13 set for scope changes and Issue #37 set for sync. The attendee-list question is answered in the same paragraph: whether the read includes attendee names and addresses is stated in the approval, priced as relationship data (Issue #17's data classes apply to the list, not just the event body), and if the answer is "the tool doesn't say", the approval records an unknown — it does not record a no.

**Rule 2 — Invite rights are a separate grant from write rights.** Creating, editing and deleting events on the owner's own calendars is one decision. Sending invitations — putting commitments into other people's calendars and inboxes, in the owner's name — is a second, separate decision, written as its own line in the approval, with its own ceiling. Sensible defaults: the agent may draft invitations freely; it may send them only inside the organisation, or only to named recurring attendees; external invitations — candidates, customers, investors, anyone outside — sit behind a human approval step, the scheduling form of Issue #30's second pair of eyes for money and Issue #32's human step for outward-facing actions, because an external invitation *is* an outward-facing action: it is correspondence, judged by the recipient as the company's voice. Where the platform cannot separate draft from send, that limitation goes in the approval record and the write tier drops to what the record can defend — the same discipline Issue #36 applied to send-as in mail.

**Rule 3 — External attendees get the bystander treatment.** Issue #29's three tests, applied to the diary. *Reach:* before approval, enumerate who is inside the grant by class — every attendee on every reachable calendar, past and future — not just the account holder. *Notice:* there is an answer that does not depend on the attendee asking: an invitation sent by an agent is marked as agent-sent where the platform allows it, and where it does not, the owner's human follow-up carries the weight for anything that matters — a first meeting, a changed time, a cancellation. A cancellation notice with no reason and no human name fails the notice test on its face, exactly as it did in Issue #29. *Refusal and remedy:* an attendee who objects to dealing with the agent gets a human, and a wrongly moved or deleted meeting has a named owner whose job is to restore it and apologise — decided in advance, not improvised by whoever finds the confused reply first.

**Rule 4 — Deletions and moves of other people's meetings are writes against bystanders, and are logged as such.** Moving or deleting an event with attendees is not housekeeping; it changes commitments other people hold. The approval sets the rule in advance — agent may move or delete only events it created, or only events with no external attendees, or none at all without approval — and the connector's log position is tested against it: can the estate show, afterwards, which agent session moved which meeting and who was on it? That is Issue #21's one-hour reconstruction test applied to the diary, and Issue #25's evidence rule prices the answer: the artefact, not a summary. If the log names only the owner for the agent's moves, the approval record says so, and the move/delete rights are set to what that record can support.

**The agent-specific line.** None of these rules is a setting on the connector. They live in the approval record — which calendars, which invite rights, which bystander treatment, which log position — which is precisely why they get missed: the connector review asked whether the tool *can* read the diary, and nobody asked whose relationships it maps or whose inbox it writes to. Price the calendar grant against the invite list it actually reaches, the way Issue #38 prices a write grant against the moderation that actually exists for the channel.

## 4. Regulator / Industry-Body Update — ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 via INCITS/AI: a participation route, open and rolling

This week's update is a route, reported at exactly that status — an open door, not a membership. It follows the same discipline as Issue #37's NIST Playbook route and Issue #38's IEEE SA route: open routes are reported as open routes, and nothing here should be read as "we joined" or "we sit on" anything.

**International AI standards work in ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 — the subcommittee for artificial intelligence — is reached from the United States through INCITS/AI, the US technical advisory structure for that subcommittee, and the participation route is virtual and hybrid, and rolling.** That is the fact at the precision the industry-bodies scan of 16 November 2026 verified: the route exists, it is the recognised channel by which US-based practitioners reach the SC 42 working groups where AI terminology, governance, risk management and trustworthiness standards are drafted, participation does not require being in a particular room, and there is no single closing date — the work proceeds in working groups that take participants on a rolling basis. The scan ranked this route ninth of eleven checked opportunities for this readership's field — below the consultations with hard deadlines and the conference calls, precisely because it is the slowest and least visible of the routes this section has covered: committee drafting, measured in years.

**No membership or participation is claimed here.** Nothing in this issue should be read as Vivek Kumar being a member of, a delegate to, or a participant in INCITS/AI, any US technical advisory group, or any ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 working group — he is not, in this newsletter's record, and the distinction is the standing rule of this section. If the route is taken, it will be reported here as a new fact with a date on it, the way actual filings and submissions have been reported in earlier issues when they happened.

Why this route, for this readership? Because SC 42 is where the vocabulary the rest of the system runs on gets fixed. The definitions of AI risk, of governance, of what an AI system's documentation must show — the language that later appears in regulation, in procurement requirements and in audit criteria — is drafted in rooms like these, by the people who showed up. The connector layer is this newsletter's standing example of a surface the vocabulary has not caught up with: an agent holding delegated access to a person's calendar, mapping their relationships and writing to their contacts, is an SC 42-shaped problem — part governance, part risk management, part trustworthiness — with products already attached. A practitioner who has run the audits brings the thing committee drafting chronically lacks: evidence from the field about what deployed systems actually document and actually do. The kit, the dataset and the method behind them are open source. The route is open. That is the whole claim this section makes.

## 5. The Monday Fix — write one calendar connection's invite rules

Forty-five minutes, this Monday — one calendar connection already live in your estate, and the four rules from Section 3:

1. Pick **one calendar connector** — the one connected to the busiest diary, not the quietest. Write down, from the approval record or the tool's settings, which calendars it can actually read, including the shared-in ones nobody listed at approval time. If the calendar list cannot be produced from the record, that is the first finding — record it as such, dated (Issue #25's rule: evidence or it didn't happen).
2. **Answer the attendee question in writing.** Does the read include attendee names and email addresses? Test it the way Teardown #46 will: ask the tool about an event it was never pointed at, on a shared calendar, and see whose names come back. File the answer in the approval record — a yes priced as relationship data, a no evidenced by the test, an "unknown" recorded as an unknown with an owner and a date to resolve it.
3. **Split the invite grant.** Write the second line into the approval: draft freely; send internally within the stated ceiling; external invitations behind a named human's approval. Walk it once, today — have the agent draft one external invitation and confirm the human step actually interrupts the send. If the platform sends without interrupting, record the limitation and drop the write tier to match, in the record, before lunch.
4. **Set the move/delete rule and the trigger.** Write which events the agent may move or delete — its own creations, no-external-attendee events, or none without approval — and set the re-approval trigger: a newly shared-in calendar, a new invite right, or a log gap found at reconstruction each reopens the approval. File the page — calendar list, attendee answer, invite rule, move/delete rule — with the connector approval. Then book the same forty-five minutes for the next calendar connection. One diary a week is slow, and it is also how an estate's invite lists actually get governed.

Score the connector before you trust it — and read the invite list before you grant the diary, because a calendar is not a schedule with an AI attached; it is a map of who matters to whom, with a pen that writes to all of them. This Monday, one connection's invite rules are the fix.
